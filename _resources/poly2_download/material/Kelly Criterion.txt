Control your Сapital on Prediction Markets with Kelly Criterion.
In this article I want to explain the Kelly formula in the simplest way possible and show how to use it the right way on Prediction Markets.
I won’t use heavy math or complicated terms. I’ll show what affects what and how you can apply it in your own trading.
I also want to say that the Kelly Criterion is just one small piece of a much bigger puzzle.
1. The basic formula of the Kelly Criterion.
The Kelly Criterion shows how big your position size should be. 
It depends on two things: your edge over the market and the potential profit the market offers.
The original formula looks like this:
F = (K × p − 1) / (K − 1)
K - odds
p - your estimated probability
F - the fraction of your bankroll for trade
In very simple words: Position size = edge / potential profit
If your edge is bigger, you buy more. If the potential profit is smaller, you also buy more. 
If there’s no edge, you don’t buy at all.
2. Simplifying the Kelly Formula for Prediction Markets.
The original version of the formula is built around traditional odds. That format is not very common on prediction markets but you can still convert prices into odds.
For example:
If the price is 25c the odds are 4.
Why? Because if you win you get 4x.
So the odds (K) = 1 / Price.
Now let’s rewrite the formula for prediction markets:


F = (p − P) / (1 − P)

p - your estimated probability
P - market price
We simplified it as much as possible.
And the logic is still the same: Position size = edge / potential profit.
3. The Logic Behind the Formula.
To really understand this formula we need to see the cause and effect inside it.
In simple words: How does the result change when the inputs change?
It’s like the speed formula: Speed = Distance / Time
If distance goes up and time stays the same -> speed goes up.
If distance stays the same and time goes up -> speed goes down.
The Kelly formula works the same way.
If your edge increases and potential profit stays the same -> bet size increases.
If potential profit increases and your edge stays the same -> bet size decreases.
We have 3 parts:
Kelly fraction (F)
Your estimated probability (p)
Market probability (P)
We observe how the Kelly fraction changes.
Situation 1:
P stays the same, your p changes:
The line is straight because this is a linear function.
This shows that when the market price is fixed, your position size grows in direct proportion to your edge.
Kelly scales confidence into position size in a linear way.
Situation 2:
Your p stays the same, P changes.
Now the line is curved because this is a rational function.
When the market moves, position size reacts in a nonlinear way.
The lower the price: the higher the potential payout and the stronger Kelly starts to "accelerate".
That’s why cheap probabilities can create very aggressive Kelly sizing.
4. Flipping the Logic.
The Kelly formula shows something interesting.
Two situations may look the same at first glance but the result can be very different.
Let’s compare.
Case 1:
P (Market Price) = 0.2
p (your estimated probability) = 0.4
That’s 2x higher. Looks strong.
Case 2:
P (Market Price) = 0.4
p (your estimated probability) = 0.8
Also 2x higher. Same logic, right?
But the Kelly fraction in these two cases is very different:
Why?
Because Kelly doesn’t just look at the ratio.
It balances two things:
how often you win
how much you win when you’re right
In the second case, you have a high probability of winning and a solid payout.
That combination makes Kelly much more aggressive.
In simple words: It’s not just about "2x better".
It’s about how often you get paid and how big the payout is.
5. Fractional Kelly and How to Use It.
Unlike the market price or odds, the Kelly formula also requires your own estimate of the outcome probability.
And that’s where risk comes in.
What if your estimate is wrong?
That’s why we have:
Full Kelly — mathematically optimal.
Fractional Kelly — practically optimal.
Here’s the logic idea: 
Full Kelly is like driving 85 mph. Fractional Kelly is like driving 65 mph. Both get you there fast. But the second one gives you a much lower chance of crashing.
How it’s used in trading:
1/2 Kelly — if the model is well tested
1/4 Kelly — standard approach
1/8 Kelly — if the market is noisy
< 1/8 Kelly — if there is tail risk
On prediction markets most people use 1/4 Kelly or less.
Example of changes on the same charts that were before
Probabilities are subjective and information shocks happen. So Pm traders should use Fractional Kelly.
Kelly is basically a formula to control greed.
Full Kelly says: "If you’re perfectly right, buy this much".
Fractional Kelly says: "You’re not perfectly right".
